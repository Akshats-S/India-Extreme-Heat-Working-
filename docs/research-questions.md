# Research questions

The handout asks for **2-3 candidate research questions** the dataset could answer. For each: the policy relevance and a technique from the course that could plausibly answer it (valuation, instrument comparison, CBA/discounting, or treatment-effect reasoning).

**Status: completed candidate applications within the registered topic.** The
descriptive temperature comparison is implemented. The applications below
specify what additional evidence would be needed; they are not claims that
cost-benefit or treatment-effect estimates have already been produced.

## Primary project question (fixed)

> To what extent has the frequency of extreme nighttime heat changed relative to extreme daytime heat across major Indian cities between 2001 and 2024?

Heat exposure creates adaptation choices about the timing, location and design
of protective services. Comparing daytime and nighttime patterns supplies
exposure evidence for those choices. It connects to public provision of
information and cooling access, budget allocation, distributional concerns,
and energy-related externalities. These are research motivations; the current
dataset measures temperatures, not welfare, mortality or electricity demand.

## Candidate question 1: the value of extending protection overnight

- **Question:** In which selected cities would extending heat-protection
  services into the night have the greatest potential net social benefit?
- **Policy relevance:** Adapting the hours and location of cooling centers,
  outreach and warnings to an exposure profile that includes high minimum
  temperatures. Distributional analysis would examine who can actually use
  the services.
- **Course technique:** Cost-benefit analysis and discounting. Compare the
  present value of incremental benefits with investment, operating and
  maintenance costs under explicit effectiveness assumptions.
- **What this dataset contributes:** City-specific nighttime exposure
  frequencies, changes over time and the timing of unusually warm dates.
  These can define exposure scenarios; they do not directly rank net benefits.
- **Additional data and assumptions:** Population and vulnerability measures,
  existing service provision, take-up, incremental service costs, causal
  effectiveness estimates, values used to monetize benefits, and a stated
  discount rate. Report sensitivity rather than treating assumed values as
  observations.

## Candidate question 2: comparing adaptation instruments

- **Question:** How should the choice between subsidized passive/efficient
  cooling and publicly provided nighttime cooling access vary with local
  daytime/nighttime exposure patterns?
- **Policy relevance:** Comparing adaptation instruments under a limited
  budget, including accessibility and the possibility that private cooling
  has energy costs or external effects.
- **Course technique:** Instrument comparison, supported by incidence and
  distributional reasoning. Compare who receives protection, implementation
  costs, incentives, coverage and externalities under each instrument.
- **What this dataset contributes:** A consistent exposure classification
  and city-period comparison for defining the policy scenarios.
- **Additional data and assumptions:** Building/housing conditions, costs and
  take-up of retrofits, access to reliable electricity, cooling-center use,
  energy savings or demand, emissions factors and effectiveness evidence.
  One-degree data cannot resolve the cooling effect of a particular roof or
  neighborhood, so such impacts must come from suitable finer-scale evidence.

## Candidate question 3: evaluating heat-action-plan outcomes

- **Question:** Did heat-action-plan adoption reduce adverse health outcomes
  on high-minimum-temperature dates relative to credible comparison cities
  and periods?
- **Policy relevance:** Assessing whether response plans protect people when
  nighttime-proxy temperatures are unusually high, rather than assuming that
  a plan's existence implies effectiveness.
- **Course technique:** Treatment-effect reasoning, potentially a carefully
  justified difference-in-differences or event-study design.
- **What this dataset contributes:** Daily weather exposure controls and
  pre-specified warm-day/night indicators. The health outcome, rather than
  temperature, would be the policy-effect endpoint.
- **Additional data and identification needs:** Reliable adoption dates and
  implementation intensity, daily health outcomes, population denominators,
  appropriate control cities and confounders. Examine pre-trends, concurrent
  policies, spillovers and reporting changes. Resolve outcome/date alignment
  for that study. The current descriptive comparison does not establish
  parallel trends or identify a causal effect.
